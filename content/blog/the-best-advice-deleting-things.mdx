A mentor sat next to me while I was explaining a feature I had built. I had spent
about two weeks on it. I was proud of it and I wanted to talk about the
architecture.

She listened to the whole explanation, then asked: "Who is using this?"

I did not have a good answer.

## The question that broke the habit

That is the whole thing. Not a technique, not a principle, just one question asked
at the right moment.

I went back and looked. Of the four things I had built that month, one had a user
touching it daily. One had a user who tried it once. Two had nobody at all, and I
had written tests for both.

I have asked that question before since, and it has a near-perfect hit rate. Every
feature I have deleted because nobody wanted it was time I did not spend on the
three people who actually needed the product. Every feature I kept, and then
polished, was because somebody asked for it out loud.

## Why building is so easy to justify

Writing code feels like work, and deleting it does not. So the default drift is
always towards more. The complexity cost of a feature is paid off slowly and
locally, by the person who has to understand it. The benefit arrives immediately
and is experienced by the person who just wrote it. That asymmetry is the whole
problem, and no amount of willpower fixes it.

What fixes it, in my experience, is making the question cheap to answer. I do not
have a good process for this. All I have is the habit of asking before I start,
and a willingness to delete things I built myself, which is the hardest part and
the only part that seems to matter.

## Deleting is a skill

There is a thing people believe about deletion that I used to get wrong. I thought
removing your own work was a loss. It is not. It is the cheapest possible outcome
for a feature that was not going to be used, and it returns a chunk of your
attention.

The projects I regret are not the ones I failed to finish. They are the ones I
finished three times over, for nobody, and then maintained for a year because
throwing it away felt like admitting something.

## The actual advice

If I could put one line in a notebook and nothing else, it would be this: before
you build, find out who wants it, and if you cannot name someone, do not start.

I still break it. I had a whole dashboard built before I asked. It is gone now,
and the week I got back was worth considerably more than the month it took.
